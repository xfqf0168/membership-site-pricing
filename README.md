## Why People Build Membership Sites (And Why Most Stall)

Membership sites are attractive for one reason: **recurring revenue**. Instead of chasing one-off sales, you collect predictable monthly or annual payments from members who stick around because they're getting ongoing value. That value can be anything — gated content, a directory of businesses, a community forum, courses, lead-gen access, or some combination of all of it.

But here's where most people stall. They start with a platform that wasn't built for memberships. WordPress plus a membership plugin sounds flexible until the plugin conflicts with your theme, your payment processor, or the latest WordPress core update. Wild Apricot and MemberClicks work, but they feel like 2008 to anyone under 35. Webflow and Squarespace make beautiful pages but have no real member system, no recurring billing logic, and no directory structure. A custom build? That's five figures and a developer on retainer.

The pattern is always the same: fragmented tools, broken integrations, and a site that sort of works until it doesn't. If you want to **build a membership site** that lasts, the platform decision matters more than almost anything else you'll do.

## Step 1: Define Your Membership Strategy Before You Touch a Platform

Before you pick a tool, answer three questions honestly:

1. **Who is this for?** Not "everyone who likes my niche" — be specific. Wedding vendors in New Jersey. Indie podcasters. Real estate agents in mid-sized cities. The narrower, the better.
2. **What do they pay for?** Access to a directory? Premium content? Leads? Community? A combination? Your revenue model flows directly from this answer.
3. **How do they find you?** If your answer is "I'll figure that out later," you don't have a business yet — you have a hobby.

The most common membership models are:

- **Single tier**: One price, everyone gets the same access. Simple to run, hard to upsell.
- **Tiered**: Multiple levels with escalating perks. More revenue potential, more complexity.
- **Freemium**: Free entry, paid premium features. Great for growth, harder to convert.
- **Per-listing or per-feature**: Members pay for specific actions (a directory listing, a featured spot, a lead). Common in directory-style sites.

Set concrete goals too. "I want $10K MRR within six months" is a target. "I want to grow the community" is a wish.

## Step 3: Design Your Site Around the Member Journey

A membership site isn't a brochure. It's a place members return to. That means design has to serve the member experience, not just look good on a landing page.

Your homepage should answer three questions in under five seconds:

- **Who is this for?** (A clear audience signal.)
- **What do they get?** (The value proposition, visible immediately.)
- **How do they join?** (An obvious call to action.)

Beyond the homepage, the onboarding flow matters more than people think. New members should be able to do something useful within their first session — update their profile, post a listing, join a discussion, book a call. ## Step 4: Set Up Recurring Payments and Pricing Tiers

This is the part that breaks on most DIY builds. Recurring payments aren't just "charge a card every month." You need:

- **Tiered pricing** with clear benefit differentiation
- **Free trial periods** to reduce signup friction
- **Discount codes and coupons** for promotions
- **Pro-rated upgrades and downgrades**
- **Failed-card retry logic** (dunning) so you don't lose members to expired cards
- **Automated invoices and receipts**
- **Tax handling** for the jurisdictions you sell into

### Pricing Tier Best Practices

If you're setting up tiers, keep them to three or four levels. More than that creates decision paralysis and administrative burden. Make the price jumps meaningful — a $10 gap between tiers isn't worth the upgrade friction. Label them clearly (Essentials, Builder, Pro — or Starter, Growth, Scale), and highlight one tier as "most popular" to anchor the decision.

## Step 6: Launch and Onboard Your First Members

A successful launch is about connection, not perfection. Here's what actually works:

1. **Tease the launch** with a countdown or preview posts to your existing audience.
2. **Use founder-led marketing** — people join people, not logos. Show your face.
3. **Seed the directory** before you open the doors. Use the instant business records so the site isn't empty on day one.
4. **Make the first action obvious.** New members should update their profile or post a listing within their first session.
5. **Follow up personally** with your first 50 members. Their feedback shapes everything.

What doesn't work: launching to crickets because you spent six months perfecting the design and forgot to build an audience first.

## Step 7: Sustain and Grow

Growth doesn't end at launch — it starts there. The membership sites that last do three things consistently:

- **Host recurring touchpoints**: monthly newsletters, member spotlights, weekly Q&As.
- **Run engagement loops**: challenges, leaderboards, member-led events.
- **Adapt the model** as members evolve. What they wanted at month one isn't what they want at month twelve.
